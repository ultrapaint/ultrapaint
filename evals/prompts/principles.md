These are the principles of the people the skills are made for. Hold every design to them, as
firmly as to the reference.

1. **Less is more.** Every element earns its place. A label, description, helper text, caption,
   heading, tooltip, badge or icon that says what the content already shows is noise, and noise
   makes a design worse, not safer. Agents add it by habit; it is the first thing to find.
   - A card titled "Total revenue" over "$41,295 total revenue"; a figure with a caption that
     names it again.
   - A description under a section heading that restates the heading ("Notifications: manage
     your notifications").
   - A line explaining a control whose label or icon already says it ("Click to upload" under an
     upload area, "Toggle to enable" beside a toggle).
   - An intro paragraph, eyebrow or subtitle the brief did not ask for, on a screen whose purpose
     is plain.
   - A group heading over a group that is obvious by its content (an "Actions" heading over two
     buttons), a label repeated on every row that a column header says once.
   - Decoration: an icon beside every label, a box around a single element, a divider between
     things already apart, a badge that says nothing.
   - An icon inside a field whose label or placeholder already names it (an envelope in an
     email field), an icon before every item of a tree or a list of names.
   - A label over a group of controls whose own words already say what they are (a
     "Workspace permissions" heading over three toggles that each name a permission).
   The fix is to remove it, not to shrink or grey it.
2. **Structure explains.** Alignment, grouping, repetition and position say what belongs
   together and what matters before any word does. When a design needs words to explain its
   structure, the structure is what is wrong.
3. **Groups are held by a surface, not by a heading.** When a screen does several jobs (a
   share dialog: a link to copy, people to invite, permissions), each job sits in a quiet card
   or panel of its own, so its edges say what belongs together; a word on top is not needed.
   A border needs room: content inside a bordered or filled surface keeps clear of its edge.
4. **Controls that live inside a sentence or a row are quiet.** An inline select that changes
   a value in place ("Can view", a role beside a person) has no box and no border: its text and
   a chevron are enough. Only a field the user is expected to fill looks like a field.
5. **Data has a hierarchy.** In a card or a row of data, one value is the headline (the name,
   the amount, the event), the rest are smaller and quieter, and labels are quieter still. Data
   set all at one size and weight is a list, not a design.
6. **Alignment is exact.** Nested items line up on one column (a new item under a group starts
   where the group's items start); icons share one style (outline or filled, one corner
   radius, one way of drawing a dot or a badge).
7. **Content leads, chrome recedes.** The data, the text and the primary action are the
   heaviest things on the screen; frames, borders, backgrounds and navigation are the quietest.
8. **Two text colours, plainly apart.** Titles, names and values in a near-black foreground;
   descriptions, hints and metadata in a muted grey that is visibly lighter. When the two read
   as nearly one grey, or a description is as dark as its title, the screen loses its second
   level.
9. **The typeface is part of the design.** The product's own face, actually loaded: a screen in
   the machine's fallback sans looks generic however good its layout is. Where a face is chosen,
   a neo-grotesque or geometric sans (Inter, Geist, Space Grotesk, Instrument Sans, Figtree) or
   a mono for a technical product; never a serif for interface text. A serif can carry the
   display headlines of a landing page or a brand site when the brand asks for one. Neutrals are neutral or
   warm, not blue-grey. (200 shadcn looks swiped: serif bodies liked 5 of 60, the blue-grey base
   0 of 23; radius and icon set made no difference.)
10. **Character follows the product.** A technical tool (admin, data, developer) is minimal and
    precise; a consumer, learning or game product is warmer and roomier, with larger navigation
    and illustrations where they mean something; a landing page or brand site is freer and more
    creative. The other principles hold in all of them.
