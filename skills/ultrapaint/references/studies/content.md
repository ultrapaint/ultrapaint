# Content on well-made sites

The numbers come from 4,983 sites: 996 technical, 982 humanistic and 3,005 expressive. A median counts only the sites that have the field. A median of 15 subhead words, for example, describes the 2,909 sites that have a subhead.

The particulars (quoted copy, patterns, pixel sizes) come from notes on 900 of those sites, 300 per character. Note-based counts are marked "in the notes" and are a sample, not a census.

Read the character skill first. This reference sets the words. The principles still decide what stays.

---

## 1. What holds across characters

### A headline of about five words

- 94% of sites have a headline (4,668 of 4,983). The median is **5 words**.
- Write it as a claim, not a label. In the notes, short stacked sentences with full stops recur across all three characters:
  - "Send it. Win it."
  - three verbs of one word each, each with its full stop;
  - "Real food. Honest guides. No fluff."
- Section titles take the full stop too: "How we compare.", "Measure your baseline."

### A subhead is one sentence, and often there is none

- 58% have a subhead (2,909). The median is **15 words**, which is one sentence.
- The other 42% (2,074) let the headline stand alone.
- Write a subhead only when it adds what the headline cannot: who it is for, the price, or how it works.
- Never write one that restates the headline in more words.

### No eyebrow by default

- 78% of sites carry no eyebrow (3,861 of 4,968).
- On the 1,107 that do, the eyebrow in the notes almost always says something the heading does not:
  - **News:** a "New" chip, the announcement and an arrow.
  - **Proof:** a rating with its score and the review site that gave it, a launch-day ranking. Write one only from a rating the product really has.
  - **A sequence:** "01 · AGENTS RESEARCH", "STEP 4".
  - **A card's category:** "FOOD HISTORY", "ARTICLE in TRAVEL".
- An eyebrow that names the section its heading already names is noise. Remove it.

### Two actions, one heavier

- 78% show at least one action (3,866). The median is **2**.
- In the notes the pair is almost never two equals. A filled primary sits beside something quieter:
  - an outline,
  - a light-grey fill,
  - or a bare text link with an arrow ("See demo", "Install with AI ›").
- The primary starts with a verb: "Get started", "Book a demo", "Download for Mac", "Start for free →", "Reserve".
- The nav may repeat the hero's primary action. Nothing else on the first screen should.

### Proof sits beside the claim, once

- In the notes, the trust line sits directly under the actions, not in a section of its own. It takes one of these forms:
  - stars and a review count,
  - an avatar stack and a number,
  - a row of greyscale logos.
- One small grey line of reassurance under the button recurs in about 20 notes:
  - "No credit card required"
  - "Free forever · No account, no sign-up"
  - "14-day trial · No credit card required"
- One line is enough. The notes also show the habit to avoid:
  - one site stated its review count in the announcement bar and again as the eyebrow;
  - others stack laurels, stars, avatars and logos on one row.
- Pick the strongest single proof.

### Real numbers, where the decision is made

- Specific figures beat adjectives. From the notes:
  - "750,000+ clients"
  - "Hosted 373,571 people in 78,388 events"
  - "There are still 1103 posts left"
- A price sits with its action:
  - in the subhead: "$29/month or $290/year";
  - in the button: "Buy a license · $39", "ADD TO BAG – $66";
  - beside a struck-through original.
- A date sits on its card: "Category · Date".

### A second level by colour, not by another line

- A headline can carry its own second level: a near-black claim, then a grey continuation at the same size.
  - "Your data has been talking. You just couldn't hear it."
  - "Set it up once. / Watch it get better."
- About 30 of the 300 technical notes do this, and a handful of the others.
- It often replaces a subhead.
- The grey must be visibly lighter (principle 8). If the two read as one grey, use one colour.

### Technical: claim, explain, prove

Technical products speak most on their own pages: the longest headlines, a subhead on 83%, three actions, and the most eyebrows.

- **Headline.** Six words, often two-tone, often two sentences. When a full-width product mock carries the screen, the headline drops to about 26–30 px and lets the mock lead (notes).
- **Subhead.** One sentence of about 19 words saying what the product does for whom. Inline product nouns can stand in for a feature list: "HTML, icons, easings, course".
- **Proof.** Logos come right after the hero. Ratings come right after the button. Case studies lead with the result figure ("$40M+", "+140%").
- **Tone.** Exact, sometimes dry and funny: "yep, all of them", "Back to building by 2:33pm". It is never vague.
- **Pricing pages** (about 16 notes):
  - median headline about 7 words, the subhead often absent;
  - 4–8 actions, one per plan, plus a billing toggle with its saving ("Save up to 24%");
  - mark the recommended plan with one signal: a tab, a fill, or the only filled button. Several notes use three at once; that is two too many.
- **Help centres** (6 notes): all six have no subhead. The headline is 2–6 words, often a question, and search is the hero.

### Humanistic: warm, specific, one way in

- **Headline.** Five words, a subhead on two in three, two actions.
- **Voice.** The voice is warmer, and the warmth is in the words:
  - puns as product names, each with a plain descriptor beside it;
  - questions answered in the body: "What's pasture raised?";
  - "you" and "your" throughout.
- **Shops** (the largest humanistic genre in the notes):
  - a card holds name, price and at most one badge ("Best Seller", "New in");
  - a sale shows the struck-through original beside the price;
  - trust claims run as one marquee line ("Free shipping · No lock-ins"), not as icon rows.
- **Restaurants and hotels** (about 24 notes):
  - the headline is usually the name, about 2 words median;
  - the subhead is usually absent;
  - one action: "Reserve", "Book a table";
  - the real content is hours, address, phone and menu, set as small tracked caps or a hairline list with right-aligned prices.
- **Games** (about 17 notes): see the title-screen recipe.

### Expressive: one word, or a whole paragraph

- Expressive copy is the shortest on average and splits in two:
  - **The wordmark is the headline.** One or two words, the name set edge to edge.
  - **A statement is the headline.** 17–72 words in the notes, set at body-plus size.
- About half have no subhead (1,576 of 3,005). A third have no action at all (1,006).
- **Portfolios** (the largest expressive genre in the notes) usually have neither:
  - the name and a one-sentence bio stand in for the headline;
  - the work is the hero, with no captions;
  - labels are plain words in the corners: "Info", "Work", "Email".
- **Editorial and magazines** (about 20 notes) repeat one stack per story:
  - a tiny uppercase kicker (often mono),
  - a serif headline,
  - a one-line dek,
  - a tracked-caps byline;
  - actions are usually none.

## 3. Recipes

### A technical product hero

Rests on the technical counts and about 25 notes with this shape.

- An eyebrow pill that carries news: a "New" chip, about six words of announcement, an arrow.
- A **6-word** two-tone headline: near-black claim, grey continuation.
- A **~19-word** one-sentence subhead.
- A filled primary with a verb ("Start for free") beside a grey-fill or text-link secondary.
- One grey line under them: "No credit card required · 14-day trial".
- A greyscale logo row directly below.
- No second subhead, no "Trusted by" heading over the logos, no icon on each reassurance.

### A pricing page

About 16 technical notes.

- A headline of about **7 words**, no subhead.
- A monthly/annual toggle with its saving written beside it ("2 months free").
- Each plan, in this order: name, one-line description, large price with a small grey unit ("/mo", "per member per month"), one button.
- The recommended plan is marked once: a "Most popular" tab, or the only filled button.
- Feature lists open with "Everything in Pro, plus:" instead of repeating rows.
- Bold the quantities inside the lines ("**10,000** API calls").

### A shop card and its page

Humanistic shops, many notes.

- The page headline is 2–6 words, with a subhead of 0–6 words.
- Each card holds:
  - a photo on a ground matching the page;
  - the name (a pun if the brand jokes, with a plain type line under it);
  - the price;
  - one corner badge only when it says something ("Best Seller", "Limited edition").
- The button carries its price: "Add to bag – $66".
- An offer modal states one offer ("15% off your first order") and has one field.

### A title screen or entry gate

About 17 humanistic game notes, plus expressive experiences.

- The headline is the title itself: **1–4 words**, often a drawn wordmark.
- No subhead.
- One action of one word: "Begin", "Play", "Start", "Enter".
- If sound matters, add one small line: "Best experienced with sound", "Use headphones". It sits by the button or in a hairline strip.
- An escape such as "Skip intro" or "Enter without sound" is the only second action.
- Nothing scrolls.

### A statement as the headline

About 16 expressive notes, mostly studios and portfolios.

- One paragraph of **17–35 words** (up to 72) is the headline.
- It sits at about **36–58 px**, regular weight, tight leading:
  - a film production company's: 22 words at about 48 px;
  - a branding agency's: 35 words at about 58 px, six lines.
- One phrase may switch to a serif italic.
- No subhead, no eyebrow, and at most one quiet action: a text link ("Let's chat"), or an underlined email field.

### An editorial front page

About 20 expressive notes.

- A masthead, then three columns:
  - a lead story with a two-thirds image;
  - a rail of headlines split by hairlines;
  - thumbnail stories.
- Every story follows one stack:
  - a 10–12 px uppercase kicker (category, sometimes with a format icon);
  - a serif headline of about 8–12 words;
  - a one-line dek;
  - a byline in tracked caps after an italic "By".
- Section titles sit between hairline rules.
- No buttons; the headlines are the links.

## Before you finish

| You see | Fix |
| --- | --- |
| A subhead that restates the headline | Delete it; 42% of sites have none |
| An eyebrow naming the section its heading names | Delete it; keep only news, proof, a number or a category |
| Two actions of equal weight | One filled with a verb; the other outline, grey or a text link |
| Proof stated twice (bar and eyebrow, stars and laurels and logos) | Keep the strongest one, under the button |
| "Thousands of happy customers" | The number: "750,000+ clients" |
| A price far from its button | Put it in the button or the sentence beside it |
| The recommended plan marked by tab, border and colour at once | One signal |
| "Learn more" alone | Name the destination, or make the card the link |
| White copy on a busy photo with no scrim | Move the copy or darken the image; legibility first |
| A template token, a broken glyph or a fallback face in the shipped copy (seen in the notes) | Real copy in the real face, checked on screen |
| A portfolio or magazine given a "Get started" button | No action; the work and the headlines are the content |
