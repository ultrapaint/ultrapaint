# Moves

A move is a device that makes a page stand out, catches attention or carries its mood: a headline
an object passes through, one accent with one job, a list dimmed but for the item in focus. Each
below was found on many well-made pages and checked on their pictures.

## Choosing

- **One or two a page, chosen for the brief.** A move answers something the brief has: the work
  to show, a voice, a product that can run on the page, a strong photo. Pick from *Fits:*, by the
  character and the kind of page. Three moves fight; the rest of the page stays plain so the move
  reads.
- **What it needs, or none.** A move whose *Needs:* the project can't meet (cut-out photos, a real
  dataset, a 3D render, copy with a voice) is left out, never faked with a placeholder.
- **Public pages more than screens.** A landing page, a portfolio or a launch takes a move in its
  hero and maybe one more down the page. An app screen takes one at most, where it costs nothing
  to use (realistic data, one reserved accent, a status dot); its task comes first.
- **A move keeps the rules.** Contrast, target sizes, reading order, reduced motion and a pause
  control hold inside it as anywhere. A move that breaks one is the wrong move for this page. A
  headline split into lines or pieces keeps a real space between its words, so it still reads as
  words when it wraps, is copied or read aloud.
- **Not the defaults that pass for moves.** One serif-italic word in a sans headline, a display
  serif on a tech product as a look, a giant type marquee, text that lights up as it scrolls, an
  eyebrow over every section: the most repeated devices of recent pages, read as a template.

## Layout

- **Offset content edge with an empty left field.** The headline, nav or lead statement starts on a hard left edge at the page centre (about x=720 of 1440) or at a one-third offset (about x=480–500), not as a centred block. The field to its left stays empty except for a small uppercase or bracketed label or wordmark, top-aligned with the content. *Fits:* expressive studio, portfolio, architecture and report pages with short statements and section intros that repeat the same edge. *Not:* copy-heavy or data-dense pages, where the empty field wastes width and shortens lines. Collapse to one column on mobile, and keep real navigation out of the label. *Needs:* a strict grid where every offset block shares exactly one left edge, and type large enough that the offset reads as intended.

- **Straddle the band seam.** Change the background colour behind a solid element rather than in a gap. A photo row, an inset card about 90% of content width or a pill button sits across the line between two bands, with a third to a half of it on each side. For example, tall images rise out of a dark hero into the light band below. *Fits:* expressive, humanistic or technical pages built from stacked full-width bands, to tie a hero to the first section or to hang a section's "see more" button on its lower edge. *Not:* more than one or two seams a page, overlaps that cover text, or band colours too close to show the change. *Needs:* contrasting bands, an element with a solid fill and a crisp edge, and section spacing that leaves room for the overlap.

- **Gallery-wall spacing.** Short pieces, such as one project image with a caption or a one- or two-line statement, are separated by 300–900px of flat empty background, up to a full viewport. Each sits alone on a narrow or centred column, so every scroll stop holds one idea. *Fits:* expressive and technical portfolios, architecture studios, premium launches and long editorial stories meant to be read slowly. *Not:* dense, comparison or conversion pages. Never let a gap fill the whole screen with no edge, colour change or scroll hint in view, or it reads as the end of the page. *Needs:* a few strong images or terse, large statements, a calm flat background and restrained type.

- **Pinned identity panel, scrolling work column.** The viewport splits in two with a 1px rule between the halves. One side, 40–60% wide or a 290–470px rail, stays fixed and holds a large-type bio or claim with contact links at its foot. The other side scrolls on its own through full-bleed project images or entry cards. *Fits:* expressive, humanistic or technical portfolios, consultancies, hotels and restaurants, where who you are should stay in view while visitors browse. *Not:* narrow screens unless it stacks; two columns of equal-weight work that compete; a fixed side with too little to fill it. *Needs:* sticky positioning or a full-height layout with an independently scrolling column, and a breakpoint that stacks the two columns.

- **Scattered mood-board layout.** Images sit on a mostly empty canvas at irregular offsets. They range from small squares at 25–30% of page width to one oversized image near 50% that bleeds off an edge. No two neighbours share a top edge, voids of a third to half a screen stay open, and each image can have one display line or caption beside or over it. A milder form uses two loose columns of similar images, each nudged sideways and down. *Fits:* expressive and humanistic photographers, branding studios, art schools and creative platforms that set a mood for slow browsing. *Not:* shops, catalogues or archives where people scan and compare, or thin image sets that the empty space exposes. *Needs:* many strong images with one look, a deliberate offset plan, and a single-column mobile fallback.

- **Low-anchored headline.** Pin the first-screen headline to the bottom left, 30–90px above the fold. It is either one grotesque line about 3–4% of viewport height or a 2–3 line uppercase block up to 6–8%. The upper 60–70% goes to full-bleed media, a pattern or bare ground. An optional small label can sit above the headline, or a short paragraph at bottom right on the same baseline. *Fits:* expressive and technical brand-led openings, such as studios, architecture, hardware, fashion and aerospace, where the image should land first. *Not:* when the message must be read before the picture, when the media is busy at its lower edge, or on short mobile screens that push the headline below the fold. *Needs:* strong media, a dark or darkened bottom edge, and a left margin shared with the nav and footer.

- **Lone object in an empty field.** Centre one product cut-out or one cover, 15–33% of viewport width, on flat white or pale grey that fills the screen, so 70–90% stays empty. Everything else is small text pinned to corners and edges: the logo, a short nav, a one-line caption or two tiny links. *Fits:* expressive product brands, magazines, campaigns and portfolios with one object worth showing like an exhibit. *Not:* objects that are weak at small size, first screens that must carry several offers, or pages where promos and pop-ups will break the field. *Needs:* one well-lit cut-out with a soft shadow or a flat cover, a seamless background, and restrained small type.

- **Newspaper rule grid.** Story columns are split by 1–2px vertical rules running the full height of the block. Stories stack between 1px hairlines, with 2–3px bars capping a column or group. Section titles sit large between two full-width rules as running heads. Cards get no borders, fills or shadows: rules and whitespace do all the grouping. *Fits:* expressive and humanistic news, magazines, journals, research publications, archives, changelogs and menus that should feel print-authoritative. *Not:* landing pages that need visual punch, sparse content that leaves rules framing nothing, or image-led grids. *Needs:* plenty of text, with headlines, kickers and short decks for at least three columns, and a weighty headline face such as a high-contrast serif or a condensed grotesque.

- **Rule-and-label section opener.** Each section opens with a dark 1–2px rule across the content width. A small uppercase label of 11–14px in sans, mono or a number sits beneath it in a left column taking the first quarter to third of the grid, and the heading and content start level with it in the next column. Or keep that narrow label column, 300–450px, beside every section, so the empty space under each label gives a quiet, ledger-like rhythm. *Fits:* expressive, humanistic and technical long pages, such as agencies, museums, hotels, résumés and reports. *Not:* very short or centred pages, or narrow mobile widths, where the labels are left out rather than stacked over each heading. *Needs:* a two-column grid with a fixed label column, and labels of one to three words.

- **Staggered offset columns.** Project images of mixed ratios, tall portrait beside short landscape, sit in 2–4 columns. Each column starts 80–350px lower or higher than its neighbour, so no two tops share a line and deliberate gaps let tiles interlock rather than stack in rows. *Fits:* expressive, humanistic or technical portfolios, studios, architecture, illustration and shop showcases with six or more images, where browsing beats strict order. *Not:* sequences, timelines, pricing or anything compared side by side; also sets of two or three images, or images that all share one ratio, where the offset just looks like a broken grid. *Needs:* at least two aspect ratios, short captions under each image, and vertical room for the gaps.

- **Ruled ledger grid.** The whole page, from the header (logo, search and cart as separate cells) through equal-width nav cells, the hero and every tile, is divided into flush cells by 1px rules. There are no gutters, shadows or radii. A heavier 2px rule or a thin labelled strip marks section breaks. Images sit full-bleed in a cell or inset by about 32px, and one or two cells turn solid accent or dark to mark the call to action. *Fits:* expressive, technical or humanistic outfitters, studios, editorial, catalogues, archives and spec or pricing pages. *Not:* soft or luxury briefs that need air, long reading, irregular content that leaves cells half-empty, or mixing rules with cards and shadows. *Needs:* a strict 4, 5 or 12-column grid, one rule weight and colour, even item counts per row, and cells that stack on small screens.

- **Sparse grid with empty cells.** Tiles snap to a strict 4–8-column grid but fill only some cells, and their column start shifts row by row, leaving large white or black voids. Captions are small, often mono codes. *Fits:* expressive fashion, art and design portfolios that want a curated, editorial feel with a rigorous system underneath. *Not:* catalogues where density and quick comparison matter, or small screens where the voids collapse into a plain list. *Needs:* art-directed imagery strong enough to stand alone in a cell, and a grid applied without exceptions.

- **Native-ratio rows on a shared line.** Images keep their native ratios at varied widths and align on one shared bottom or top line, so the opposite edge steps unevenly. Captions sit along the shared line. *Fits:* expressive and humanistic art, photography and portfolio pages where cropping would harm the work. *Not:* uniform product catalogues, where every item has the same ratio and the effect disappears. *Needs:* original, uncropped images in genuinely different proportions.

- **Live preview beside controls.** Settings, a list or a command panel take about 40% of the screen. The rest keeps rendering the actual result and updates as each control changes. *Fits:* technical builders, editors, template pickers and command palettes, where users need to see the effect of a choice before committing. *Not:* narrow screens, or settings with no visual result to show. *Needs:* a renderer fast enough to update on every change.

## Type

- **Crushed display leading.** A large headline or intro statement, about 60–130px over 2–7 lines, is set at a line-height of 0.9–1.0 with tight or slightly negative tracking. Descenders and the caps below nearly touch, never overlap, so the text reads as one solid block. It runs flush across the full width at the top or centred as the main claim, with small, loosely set text nearby for contrast. *Fits:* expressive, humanistic or technical poster-like openings for agencies, fashion, events, magazines and confident product pages. *Not:* thin or wide faces that look gappy, text below display size, long copy, or lines thick with deep descenders and accents. Set a tighter mobile size so wraps stay controlled. *Needs:* a dense display face, such as a heavy or condensed grotesque in caps or a tightly fitted serif, and a short set of lines.

- **Grid-indented display paragraph.** An intro statement at display size, about 40–80px over 3–6 lines across most of the width, starts its first line at an inner grid column, typically 15–30% across, lined up with the nav links, a caption column or a header label. Every following line returns flush left, so the indent reads as a tie to the grid. *Fits:* expressive and humanistic studio, agency, consultancy and portfolio pages, as one positioning statement per page. *Not:* body copy or multiple paragraphs, mobile widths where the first line holds a word or two (drop or shrink the indent), or layouts where nothing else sits on that column. *Needs:* a visible column grid with another element at the indent, a large face with comfortable leading, and space above and below.

- **Full-width wordmark masthead.** The name is set on one line and scaled to span the full viewport or content width, roughly 100–250px tall. It either stands alone in white space as the whole first screen or caps an editorial grid like a masthead. A thin row of tiny nav or account links runs along its top edge, with an optional row of section links under it. *Fits:* expressive and humanistic studios, portfolios, magazines, fashion labels, restaurants and events with a short, confident name. *Not:* long or multi-word names that shrink or wrap, or products that must explain themselves on the first screen. *Needs:* a short name, a display face that holds up huge (a heavy grotesque, an extended sans or a condensed high-contrast serif), a quiet ground, and type sized fluidly to the viewport width.

- **Giant stat numerals.** One to three key figures are set as huge numerals, 120–260px, in a high-contrast serif or a light grotesque. Each has a small raised unit and a 2–3 line body-size caption directly below. A lone figure sits left with ample space around it; three sit in equal columns, each under a hairline rule, on a plain or dark band. *Fits:* expressive, humanistic or technical research reports, funds, agencies and product pages with a few striking real figures. *Not:* small, vague or vanity numbers, too many stats, or figures without a caption that says what they count. *Needs:* real, sourced figures, a caption for each, and a face whose numerals look good large.

- **Images set inline as words.** In a large display headline, about 6–10vw in caps, small photos, video clips or cut-out figures sit in the text flow at cap height. A rectangle is 1–1.5em wide and a cut-out stands as tall as the capitals. They sit between words or fill the empty ends of stepped rows, reading like words in the sentence. *Fits:* expressive and humanistic hero or section statements for agencies, studios and consumer brands with strong photography. *Not:* busy, low-contrast pictures that blur at 1em, small mobile widths, or headlines whose meaning breaks for screen readers. Give decorative images empty alt text, or keep a plain-text version. *Needs:* small high-contrast photos, short loops or cut-outs, and a heavy display face whose cap height they match.

- **Monospace label layer.** Every small functional label, including nav, eyebrows, breadcrumbs, button text, tags, dates, status lines and captions, is set in 11–14px uppercase monospace. Tracking is wide, about 0.1–0.2em, often in a muted tone, with slashes or bullets as separators. Headlines stay in a large grotesque or serif at 4–10× that size, so the page has an editorial voice and an instrument-panel voice. *Fits:* technical developer, AI, fintech and hardware products; expressive studios and media; humanistic craft and beauty brands wanting a lab-label look. *Not:* paragraphs in mono caps, soft or playful brands, or labels so small and faint that they fail as navigation. *Needs:* a monospace face that tracks well in caps, a contrasting display face, a strict rule for which roles get mono, and a muted secondary colour.

- **One-size type system.** All text, from name and nav to bio, captions and lists, is set at one size of about 16–18px, usually in one face, often a monospace or a plain grotesque. Rank comes only from full contrast versus grey, a bold word, grid column and blank lines. Any large lettering lives inside the work's images. *Fits:* expressive and technical portfolios, studio pages and archives with very little copy, where the grid and images set the scale. *Not:* editorial, content-heavy or conversion pages that need scannable headings or a loud call to action. *Needs:* a disciplined multi-column grid with fixed column starts, a clear primary-versus-grey contrast step, and generous whitespace or strong imagery.

- **Statement paragraph interlude.** Directly after the hero, in its own band with generous space above and below, a short point-of-view paragraph is set at 36–56px across 70–95% of the content width, in a light display face or a bold serif. An optional 11–12px uppercase eyebrow can sit above it. It comes before any cards or grids and reads as a second headline. *Fits:* expressive, humanistic and technical brand, agency, hospitality, place, wellness and software home pages that should pause and state a view. *Not:* copy over three or four lines at this size, repeating the hero's message, or placement over busy images. *Needs:* one or two sharp sentences of real positioning copy, and a face that spaces well large.

- **Two-size extreme scale.** The first screen uses two sizes only: a display line of 100px or more, up to an edge-to-edge wordmark, and 11–13px tracked uppercase labels for nav, captions and callouts, pinned to corners or tucked beside the display. There is no middle size, and wide empty space makes the gap the hierarchy. *Fits:* expressive and humanistic opening screens for portfolios, studios, furniture, fashion and other gallery-like brands with very little copy. *Not:* first screens carrying feature lists, pricing or reading text. Lower down, bring back a middle size for body content rather than forcing it into label size. *Needs:* a very short headline or name, plenty of space, and labels with enough contrast to read at 11–13px.

- **Two-tone sentence.** A heading or intro is set at one size and weight but in two tones. Either the lead claim is near-black and the rest runs on in mid-grey, or the sentence is grey and only its keywords lift to full contrast, so the dark words alone read as a skim summary. It scales from a 15–18px intro up to a 48–96px opening line, set left or centred. *Fits:* expressive, humanistic or technical hero statements, about-me openers and intro blurbs on agency, portfolio, software and developer pages that want hierarchy without a subtitle. *Not:* grey below 4.5:1 contrast (especially on photos), copy that doesn't split into lead and continuation, or so many lifted words that they stop forming a phrase. *Needs:* copy written so the dark part stands alone.

- **Frame-cropped giant wordmark.** The name is set so large, with letters 25–60% of viewport height and wider than the screen, that a viewport edge or the hero's lower edge cuts the letters. It sits behind or over full-bleed photo or video, or under a floating product render, as a single line clipped at both sides or two rows with the first cut by the top edge. *Fits:* expressive and humanistic portfolios, studios, campaigns and launches where the name is already known or works as texture. *Not:* a first meeting with an unknown name, crops that spell an awkward word, or a wordmark that pushes the real headline and call to action down. *Needs:* a short, heavy or very wide wordmark, an edge to crop against, and the full name shown readably elsewhere.

- **Staggered headline.** A 2–4 line headline breaks into lines or single words at 10–25% of the viewport width, each shifted to a new horizontal position. Lines step like a staircase, jump between edges, or rise a word onto a higher baseline, so the block spans the full width. Tiny bracketed mono labels, a short caption or small cut-out figures fill the gaps. *Fits:* expressive, humanistic or technical poster-like openings for agencies, conferences, exhibitions, year-in-review pages and portfolios built around a name, year or 3–6 word claim. *Not:* long or must-scan headlines, dense product pages, or mobile widths where the offsets collapse into a ragged stack. *Needs:* a very short headline, a heavy or condensed display face at a huge size, and generous space.

- **Headline split to frame content.** A name or two-word headline is split, with its halves pushed to the far left and far right (32–280px type). The gap in the middle holds a tagline, a face, a symbol or a second headline. *Fits:* expressive personal names, two-word brands and event posters with something worth framing in the centre. *Not:* splits where the halves stop reading as one name, or narrow screens where the gap closes. Stack the halves there instead.

- **Display-size quote.** One testimonial or client quote is set at 28–72px, often in a serif on an otherwise sans page, centred or left-aligned. A hairline can run under every line like ruled paper, with the next quote cropped at the edge. Or let the quote take the headline slot itself at 38–96px with hanging quote marks, a small portrait, credit or greyed publication logo below. *Fits:* humanistic and expressive pages where one strong endorsement should carry a section. *Not:* long or generic quotes that become a wall of large text. *Needs:* a short, specific, attributable quote.

- **Knockout label tags.** Short text, such as a title, dates, a logo or section labels, is set in white bold or condensed type reversed out of solid black boxes padded only 0.1–0.2em. Small tags are 20–40px tall and sit in a margin column. A large title can stack 3–4 lines with the box hugging each line, and a stack of tags can index dates or venues. *Fits:* expressive festivals, events, journals, merch and portfolios wanting a raw, label-maker or printed feel, including over busy imagery. *Not:* running text, which becomes heavy black slabs, or use on so many elements that tags stop marking anything. *Needs:* a heavy legible face such as a condensed grotesque, and copy short enough for one or a few lines.

## Colour

- **Paired-ink colour bands.** The page stacks as flat full-bleed bands, each 60–100% of the viewport tall, with hard edges and no rules or shadows. Each ground has one fixed text ink and at most one accent ink (dark navy on peach, cream on olive), and the pairing repeats wherever that ground returns. Bands can split into two half-width grounds or shrink into stacked list rows. Or push it louder: saturated poster bands at least a screen tall or 8%-tall slogan strips, butting against full-bleed photos, each opening with an 80–130px flush-left condensed heading. *Fits:* expressive, humanistic and technical long single pages for studios, agencies, hospitality and lifestyle brands. *Not:* improvised pairings, over five grounds, bands refilled with bordered cards, or low-contrast body text. *Needs:* a fixed palette of 3–5 contrast-checked grounds with their inks, and few, substantial sections.

- **Colourless chrome, work carries the colour.** Every interface element uses one neutral ground (black, charcoal or white), its opposite for text, and grey for tags, pills and hairlines, with no accent even on links or buttons. The work runs large and colourful, with edge-to-edge tiles or images taking half the width or more. Or keep black-on-white chrome above a full-bleed grid of 2–4 columns, mixed tile sizes and 15–30px gutters, with tiny labels or none. *Fits:* expressive and humanistic portfolios, agencies, galleries and publishers with vivid, varied work. *Not:* sparse, small or near-monochrome imagery, or pages where calls to action need a colour cue. *Needs:* a steady supply of large saturated images, strong type contrast, and links marked by shape or weight.

- **Item-sampled colour.** Each tile in a grid takes one colour from its own content. That colour is either a flat ground filling the tile behind a single mark or cover, or a bold title set in the image's dominant hue. The surrounding page stays neutral, so all the palette comes from the items. *Fits:* expressive and humanistic portfolios, book or record catalogues, product ranges and print shops where each item has a signature colour. *Not:* items with similar or muddy colours, grounds that fail contrast with their mark, grids of dozens of tiles that turn to patchwork, or a strong competing brand palette. *Needs:* one curated colour stored with each item rather than sampled at render, dark and light ink options, and a neutral frame.

- **Hue-keyed groups.** Each of 3–5 sibling groups (sections, audiences, product lines) gets its own saturated hue, used only on small accents. Examples are a 2–3px top rule, a 10–12px small-caps eyebrow, bullet dots, a 32–40px icon tile in a nav pill, and the tag that opens that group's section lower down. The hue recurs wherever the group appears. *Fits:* expressive, humanistic and technical pages where readers follow a group from menu to section: bios, multi-product sites, festival tracks. *Not:* more than five groups, colour as the only cue, tinted body text or whole backgrounds, or hues that appear in one place only. *Needs:* hues of similar lightness that each pass contrast, neutral text and grounds, and one mapping across every component.

- **Lone reserved accent.** Build from black, white and greys, and give one saturated hue a single job used nowhere else. That job is a highlighter-like mark about a cap-height wide beside the name, or the text links plus a thin top rule, or only the primary buy or sign-up buttons, ideally echoing the product's own colour. *Fits:* expressive and technical restrained portfolios, studios and launches wanting one memorable signal and a clear path. *Not:* spreading the hue over headings, icons and backgrounds, adding a second accent, or image-heavy pages where colourful pictures drown it. *Needs:* a strictly neutral base, and a hue that passes contrast wherever it colours text or buttons.

- **Single-ink typography.** All text, rules, icons, outline buttons and the logo are one dark non-black ink (olive, teal, terracotta, cobalt) on one off-white or lightly tinted ground. Hierarchy comes only from size and weight, from huge display lines down to about 14px captions. Secondary labels use a 50–60% tint of the ink, and a solid ink block such as the logo tile can reverse the ground out of it. Photos keep their colour, with at most one other accent fill. *Fits:* expressive, humanistic or technical bookshops, design associations, hospitality, wellness and small food brands with editorial headlines, lists and directories. *Not:* state-heavy interfaces needing semantic colours, inks too light for body text, several coloured panels, or stray neutral greys. *Needs:* an ink at 4.5:1 on the ground, and a family with a wide weight range.

- **Palette lifted from the hero image.** Page grounds and panels are flat colours eyedropped from the key photo. Either a full-width band, about 60% of the first screen, takes the subject's main colour with the photo inside it, or a side column about a quarter wide splits into blocks of the main colour and its single accent. The accent goes on the headline and call to action, leading the eye from photo to button. *Fits:* expressive and humanistic launches, food and drink, fashion, editorial features and photographers with shoots art-directed around one or two colours. *Not:* mixed or stock photography, carousels whose colour changes per slide, full-bleed photos with no separate ground, or sampled colours that fail text contrast. *Needs:* art-directed imagery with a dominant hue and ideally one accent object, and real space beside it for the solid colour.

- **Spot-ink print system.** The whole page uses one saturated ink on a plain ground, such as blue on white or green on khaki, for all type, rules, grid borders, line illustration, and photos converted to duotone. An optional second spot ink handles small accents like an outlined heading, and full-width bands such as the footer can invert to solid ink. *Fits:* expressive and humanistic portfolios, studios, fashion and cultural labels and print-minded brands that want a strong signature. *Not:* shops or photography-led pages needing true colour, or any page where one full-colour photo would slip in and break the illusion. *Needs:* imagery and logos convertible to one-ink duotone or line art, an ink dark enough for body text, and no extra greys or hues.

- **Scroll-length hue gradient.** The page background is one vertical gradient stretched over the full scroll height, stepping through 3–5 saturated hues, so each screen sits on its own colour. Content floats on it with no dividers or panels, and all text stays one colour, usually white. Or shift more slowly, blending pale to pale over several screens, or swap a saturated colour and a pale tint between two half-width columns at a section boundary. *Fits:* expressive, humanistic or technical portfolios, event series, launches and narrative showcases where scrolling should feel like a journey. *Not:* docs, utilities or dense reading, or pale and neon stops under white text (flip the text there). *Needs:* a gradient sized to document height or scroll-driven colour, and every stop checked against the text colour.

- **Single-hue tonal palette.** One brand hue fills the page at a very light or very dark value. All type, rules, labels and buttons are a contrasting tint or shade of that same hue, never black or white, with a solid fill of the ink as the one primary button. Keep a lightness gap of about 60–70 points between ink and ground, and let photos and the logo break the palette. *Fits:* expressive and humanistic calm brands such as studios, think tanks, indie apps and material suppliers, on sparse type-led pages. *Not:* mid-tone steps for small text, pages crowded with full-colour photos or embeds, or several competing calls to action. *Needs:* a hue that works both deep and pale, and checked body-size contrast.

- **Single saturated flood.** The whole page, edge to edge through every section, is one flat saturated colour, such as cobalt, hot pink, coral or acid yellow, with no white or grey bands. Type is only white and/or near-black. Cards are hairline outlines in a lighter tint or blocks in a darker shade of the same hue, never neutral boxes. *Fits:* expressive, humanistic or technical agency, campaign, launch and personal sites with a few short sections and a confident brand colour. *Not:* long or content-heavy pages, dashboards and docs, greyed or muddy colours that read as plain, or white text on light pastels. *Needs:* one strong hue, text that passes contrast on it, and little enough content that outlines and tints can structure it.

- **Single-hue data encoding.** Charts use one hue in graded tints instead of a multi-colour series palette. Examples are violet bars with faded flows, heatmap cells whose depth follows value, or a separate single hue per chart. *Fits:* technical analytics dashboards and data stories where magnitude matters more than category and the interface should stay calm. *Not:* charts that must separate many unordered categories, where tints become impossible to tell apart. *Needs:* data where order or magnitude is the message, and a hue with enough steps between pale and deep.

- **Colour-coded category markers.** Each category, type or state gets its own hue on a small marker, such as a 40–48px tile, a stripe, an icon or a gradient panel. The same code repeats in palettes, filters and legends, so colour becomes a reading key. *Fits:* technical builders, report libraries, comparisons and data tools where users must recognise item types at a glance across views. *Not:* more categories than can be told apart, or colour as the only cue for colour-blind users. *Needs:* a fixed category-to-hue map shared by every view, with a label or icon beside each colour.

- **Saturated closing footer.** The footer, with links, legal text and sometimes a big closing line or mark, sits on one flat saturated field such as mustard, burnt orange, neon green or purple, and can fill most of a viewport after a neutral page. Or place a single saturated band mid-page carrying one message, like a countdown, a stat or a press quote, with logos tinted a darker shade of the band. *Fits:* expressive and humanistic content and brand sites with calm bodies that want a confident sign-off and a clear end. *Not:* colours with weak contrast against small link text, or pages already loud above. *Needs:* a calm, neutral page above it so the field lands.

## Imagery

- **Work-first opening screen.** The first screen is the work itself. That can be one full-bleed project image or slideshow covering the viewport (or all of it beside a thin sidebar), or large thumbnails about 45% wide starting under a slim header. It can also be a looping video, a mosaic of 5–8 video tiles with narrow gutters, or one oversized frame tilted a few degrees on a dark ground with the next peeking in below. There is no headline or intro: only a small logo, tiny corner nav, slideshow dots, at most a project caption. *Fits:* expressive and humanistic studios, photographers, directors, architects and galleries whose visitors already know roughly what they do. *Not:* weak or uneven imagery, offers that need a sentence of explanation, or first screens that must convert. *Needs:* strong, high-resolution work in a consistent style, and chrome legible over it.

- **Branded argument chart.** A custom chart in the brand's palette and shapes carries the page's main claim. Examples are a radial year-clock of tiles, a dot funnel narrowing through colour-coded stages to one circled outcome, or muted bars ending in one accent bar. It runs half to full content width beside or under the one-line claim it proves, with sparse small-caps labels and one callout. *Fits:* humanistic and technical data journalism, pharma, sustainability, consulting and research-led products with real quantifiable data. *Not:* decorative pseudo-data, invented curves or unscaled funnels, points a plain number makes faster, or charts that need a legend. *Needs:* a real dataset with honest scales, one highlighted accent for the verdict, and a caption stating the claim.

- **Faint flow diagram as hero image.** A low-contrast diagram of 3–6 rounded tiles, each about 150×50px with a small icon and a one-word role, is joined by dashed or thin orthogonal connectors. Each connector carries a 2–3 word grey label naming the step. It fills the hero's image half, about 30–40% of the width, or spreads as a faint network behind a centred text column. *Fits:* technical infrastructure, automation, integration and protocol products whose value is how systems connect. *Not:* products with an interface worth showing, flows needing more than six nodes or crossing lines, labels too faint to read, or connectors that explain nothing. *Needs:* an SVG or HTML diagram of the real workflow, with small mono or small-caps labels tuned to the ground.

- **Contact-sheet wall.** Uncaptioned images tile edge to edge in a strict grid with 0–2px gutters, from five columns of large crops to 12–14 columns of thumbnails, under a slim header. Uniform cells and optionally one treatment such as greyscale make the set one surface. Or fill the full width in 3–5 equal portrait or masonry columns with 0–8px gutters and the wordmark over the top row, so the first screen is all image. *Fits:* expressive studios, photographers and archives with dozens to hundreds of images, where volume and range matter. *Not:* fewer than about 20 items, images needing captions, or items that must be compared by detail. *Needs:* a large set, a consistent cell ratio, and each tile linking to a detail view or labelled on hover.

- **Imagery graded to the palette.** Every photo shares one backdrop colour or colour grade taken from the interface. All portraits might be shot on one deep backdrop matching the text and rule colour, or every image washed in the page tint on that same ground. It holds across a whole grid or band, so images read as part of the colour system. *Fits:* expressive portrait directories, team pages, fashion, hospitality, portfolios and editorial one-offs with a signature colour. *Not:* user-generated, stock or ever-growing image sets that can't be graded alike, or tints that hide product colour buyers must judge. *Needs:* an art-directed shoot on one seamless backdrop or a consistent grade, and a restrained palette of one ground and one ink.

- **Leader-line callouts.** Hairline leaders, usually with one 45° elbow, run from 10–16px square or ring hotspots on a cut-out render, product shot or map. Each ends at a 2–4 line label of 10–13px mono or caption type in the surrounding empty space, often with a rule under the label or a larger stat on top. Three to five callouts around the subject turn the hero into a technical drawing or specimen plate. *Fits:* technical, humanistic or expressive heroes built on one isolated object, collections with named items, and maps or screenshots. *Not:* small screens where lines cross text (stack labels or show them on tap), busy full-bleed photos, or labels over a few words. *Needs:* a subject on a plain ground, a few short real facts, and leaders placed per breakpoint.

- **Logo glyph as recurring ornament.** The logo's simple symbol returns in a few roles. It can be a huge thin outline, 30–40% of the viewport, in a tint close to the ground and cropped by the hero edge behind the headline; a centred 40px glyph dividing sections; or a brand-coloured bullet before key paragraphs. *Fits:* expressive, humanistic or technical blogs, small organisations, hotels and quiet editorial sites with a compact symbol (heart, star, asterisk) and no illustration budget. *Not:* wordmark-only or detailed logos, more than two or three roles, tiling into wallpaper, or a backdrop strong enough to hurt text over it. *Needs:* a single-colour glyph that reads both as a thin outline and at bullet size, and one brand accent.

- **Object woven through the headline.** A short heavy display headline, one or two lines spanning 60–95% of the hero, is layered with a cut-out photo or render. The object passes behind some letters and in front of others, or covers the lower third of a word, hiding at most a quarter of any letter. Or set a wordmark edge to edge across the top of the first screen, with letters 15–25% of viewport tall, and stand a cut-out below it rising over the bottom 10–30% of one or two letters, on a ground in the product's colour. *Fits:* expressive launches, portfolios, agencies, events and athlete or musician heroes with one strong object and one to four words. *Not:* long headlines, rectangular photos with backgrounds, objects over the call to action, or text simply on top. *Needs:* clean cut-outs, layered front and back masks, a very heavy face, and a mobile plan.

- **Product on a page-tone ground.** The product is cut out onto the page's own background, or shot in a one-hue set of walls, plinth and floor that bleeds into the page, so it has no card, border or photo edge. Use it as one hero object at 40–60% of viewport width on a plinth or ground line, or as a row of 3–4 same-scale cut-outs about 15% wide with short captions. *Fits:* humanistic and expressive shops and launches for food, skincare, jewellery, packaging or premium cards, with one strong ground tone. *Not:* catalogues of mixed-source photos, products whose colour matches the backdrop, or long grids needing tile edges. *Needs:* art-directed cut-outs with consistent scale and shadow, or a one-hue set matched to the page colour.

- **Signature shape photo masks.** Hero or feature photos are cropped into one shape that means something for the brand, such as an arch, a running track or the logo's glyph. They are sized at 30–95% of the viewport, centred or bled off an edge. The shape repeats as thin outline frames, an oversized coloured section panel, or the path a headline runs along. *Fits:* expressive, humanistic or technical hospitality, interiors, food, sport media and branding pages where a few large images carry the mood. *Not:* several unrelated shapes, crops that cut faces or products, long curved text, or dense thumbnail grids where the shape gets lost. *Needs:* one shape drawn from the identity, photos with centred subjects, and at least one more place where the shape repeats.

- **Subject breaks the frame.** A cut-out subject sits in a card, circle mask, colour block or mock window, with part of it running past the edge. It might rise 10–25% of its height over the top, hang below, or spill splashes and limbs sideways. Or centre it on the seam between two colour bands to tie the sections together. *Fits:* expressive and humanistic product, food, drink, app and brand pages needing depth inside a tidy grid, on a hero or one block per screen. *Not:* every card doing it, overflow covering text or controls, or busy rectangular photos that can't be cut cleanly. *Needs:* transparent-background cut-outs, renders or illustrations, and layering that lets the subject sit above the frame edge.

- **Tilted paper artefacts.** Photos or content become one recognisable object: a photo-booth strip, a white-bordered print, a newsprint sheet, a magnet tile. Each is rotated 3–15° with a soft shadow on a flat page, as a centrepiece 10–20% wide or scattered around the hero headline. Or render the whole hero or each card as a cutting mat, ruled paper, a clipped kraft folder or a stamped postcard at 50–100% width, with real content pasted onto it a degree or two off. *Fits:* expressive and humanistic portfolios, newsletters, merch and print shops, restaurants and events where the object carries meaning. *Not:* enterprise or data products, imagery to compare, steeply angled body copy or forms, or clutter from too many pieces. *Needs:* styled cut-outs with borders, texture and shadows, and a calm flat ground.

- **UI crop bleeding off the card edge.** The screenshot or line sketch in each feature card is offset and clipped by the card's bottom and right edges. It reads as a window onto a larger tool rather than a shrunken screen. *Fits:* technical feature tiles and promo cards, where a full screen at card size would be unreadable. *Not:* crops that cut away the part that matters. *Needs:* screenshots or sketches with their key detail placed toward the top-left of the visible area.

- **Rebuilt miniature UI vignette.** Each feature card ends in a small fragment of the product rebuilt in HTML, such as a list, a form, a chart, a status timeline or benchmark bars. It often bleeds off the card's bottom edge and has one coloured element, like a send button or a confirmation pill, instead of an icon or full screenshot. *Fits:* technical software feature grids that want crisp, honest product detail at card scale. *Not:* vignettes that drift from the real interface and promise features the product lacks. *Needs:* someone who keeps the fragments in step with the shipped product.

- **Before/after split.** One image shows two states of the same subject through a vertical tear, a ragged spreading reveal, or two side-by-side frames tagged with and without, or versus. *Fits:* expressive and technical products that visibly transform something, such as rendering, restoration, video quality or a clarified process. *Not:* differences too subtle to see at a glance, or a "before" made deliberately bad. *Needs:* a matched pair of images of the same subject, framing and lighting.

- **Templated house cover art.** Every article or post thumbnail follows one template, such as a cut-out portrait on flat colour, an engraving collage or a 3D render with the title, so the index reads as one series. *Fits:* expressive, humanistic or technical blogs, news and resource libraries that publish often and need a consistent index. *Not:* templates so rigid that posts can't be told apart. *Needs:* a repeatable art template and someone who produces a cover for every post.

- **Edge-bleeding work strip.** One or more rows of project tiles, screenshots or renders run edge to edge, offset from each other and cropped off both sides of the viewport, implying more beyond the frame. *Fits:* expressive portfolios and technical template galleries or app showcases with plenty of consistent work. *Not:* only a few items, or items that each need to be seen whole. *Needs:* enough images in one consistent style to fill the rows several times over.

- **Product UI on scenic backdrop.** Screenshots or app windows float centred in a large rounded panel filled with a painting, landscape photo, material texture or glossy wallpaper, which shows around their edges instead of a flat gradient. *Fits:* technical and humanistic software marketing that wants calm or warmth around dense interface and a recognisable house look across feature sections. *Not:* backdrops that compete with a busy interface, or that have nothing to do with the product's mood. *Needs:* a set of backdrops with one shared tone, used consistently across sections.

## Words

- **Deadpan self-aware copy.** Copy at display size or in the body undercuts itself with a straight face. Examples are an absurdly inflated claim set as a large serif hero line, a bio that assumes the reader doesn't care, a buzzword struck through and replaced by the plain word in a heading, or a small stamp or ticker admitting a flaw. The typography stays serious so the joke reads dry, not wacky. *Fits:* expressive, humanistic and technical studios, freelancers, copywriters and challenger brands whose personality sets them apart, in heroes, about sections and headings. *Not:* health, finance, legal or other high-stakes contexts, or visitors needing fast plain facts. *Needs:* a confident writer, a restrained setting, and plain wording for contact and other actions.

- **List set as a running sentence.** Services, clients or credits run as one text block of a few wrapped lines, from body size up to 32–48px. Items are split by light slashes, dashes or commas and the block ends in a full stop. Linked items stand out by colour, underline or weight, and the last item can be a link to more or to contact. *Fits:* expressive and humanistic studios, agencies, PR firms and personal or CV sites that want an editorial voice instead of a logo grid or bullets. *Not:* lists people must sort, compare or filter, items with their own descriptions or numbers, or lists long enough to become a wall. *Needs:* about 5–15 names, and a lighter separator glyph.

- **Roster stacked as a poster.** The main content is a list of names or project titles, one per centred line, in large caps: a light high-contrast serif at 36–48px or a condensed grotesque at 80–100px. Leading is so tight that lines nearly touch, and the ragged edges form a poster shape. Each name links to its page. A small-caps role or a one-line-high thumbnail alternating sides can sit beside each. *Fits:* expressive agencies, production companies, studios and festivals with 15 or more recognisable or well-sounding names. *Not:* short or unknown lists, run-in small text, or lists over two screens without thumbnails or a filter. *Needs:* a long list where each name has its own page.

- **Paragraph as the hero.** A first-person bio or manifesto of 30–80 words, in one to three paragraphs, is set at 45–100px as the first thing on the page, replacing the headline. Employer, project and contact names are inline links marked by colour, a contrasting weight or face, or small arrows. Or set a 3–9 line serif paragraph at 40–80px with 1.05–1.15 leading across 75–85% of the width from a wide left margin, nav shrunk to edge labels, and a few phrases lifted by colour, underline, italics or inline marks. *Fits:* expressive and humanistic portfolios, independent practitioners, academics, small studios and nonprofits with a real voice. *Not:* generic copy, products that need a demo or feature list, or bios over four display lines. *Needs:* tight writing, and a link style that reads large.

- **Believable domain data in mockups.** Fill hero and feature mockups, usually a framed panel two-thirds to full width under the headline, with specific invented data from the field. Use amounts to the cent, IDs and meter names in monospace, dated document titles, version numbers and statuses that vary by row. Let the data tell a story where possible, for example browser tabs tracing the user's problem or a finished memo with real deal terms. *Fits:* technical landing pages for expert buyers in finance, billing, developer tooling and agency software, who will read the numbers. *Not:* real customer names or figures, jargon a buyer can't follow, totals that don't add up, or data so dense that the key screen disappears. *Needs:* domain knowledge to write plausible values, and a coded or high-resolution mockup so small text stays readable.

- **Signed first-person letter.** The page or a section is a first-person letter in a narrow column of about 512px or a card. It is written in short paragraphs and ends with an italic sign-off or a handwritten signature image 140–180px wide instead of a button. It can also be set in columns with a date and reference block. *Fits:* humanistic founder-led companies, launches, manifestos and about pages where honesty and a human voice replace marketing tiers. *Not:* pages that must convert fast or compare features, or fake intimacy from a large company. *Needs:* a real author, their genuine signature, and a letter they actually wrote.

## Navigation

- **Corner-label frame.** Instead of a nav bar, six or fewer tiny labels (11–14px, uppercase, sometimes a bold grotesque) are pinned 16–70px in from the four corners and often top and bottom centre. They hold the name, a few links, contact and a handle, or a name split word by word along the bottom of the first screen. Or turn them into HUD-like 10–12px micro-text: logo and local time top-left, a one-line availability note bottom-left, a scroll cue or index counter bottom-right. *Fits:* expressive and humanistic portfolios, studios, cafés and launches whose first screen is one strong visual, with three to five destinations. *Not:* content-heavy sites, audiences needing obvious navigation, busy imagery under the corners, or a single top row of labels on its own. *Needs:* high-contrast type in one style, a strong centre, and generous padding.

- **Sentence as navigation.** The first screen opens with a one- to three-sentence bio or statement in a 36–48px serif, about 45–55% wide at top left. Its key words are underlined links to two to five sections, and any menu shrinks to a utility bar. Or set the opening statement at 40–56px across about 80% of the width so that it ends by naming the page's actions, such as supporting, subscribing or starting, as thick-underlined phrases with no buttons nearby, and repeat the trick in body-size bios further down. *Fits:* expressive and humanistic writers, designers, small studios and arts organisations with a distinct voice. *Not:* more than five destinations, weak copy, shops, or returning visitors scanning fast; never use vague link words. *Needs:* copy that names each destination naturally, one consistent underline style, and fallback footer links.

- **Business-card header columns.** In place of a nav bar, three or four small text blocks of 12–14px start on the page's grid columns, 20–50px from the top. Each is a short label over a value: name and role, city, current availability, contact links. One line is dark and the other muted, and at most one small action sits at the far right. *Fits:* expressive personal portfolios and small studios on a strict column grid, with little or no navigation, where who, where and whether they're available matters most. *Not:* sites with real navigation depth, products needing a prominent header button, or narrow screens where the columns can't stack cleanly. *Needs:* a visible column grid, and short, factual copy per block.

- **Punctuated-sentence nav.** The 4–6 nav links are set as one line of 20–36px display text, joined by commas and ending on a full stop or trailing comma, in a corner or centred under the wordmark. The current item is marked typographically, either dimmed and struck through or given a thick accent underline. Tiny mono index numbers can sit above each word. *Fits:* expressive typographic studios, portfolios, publishers and editorial brands with a handful of sections, where the nav should carry a voice. *Not:* many destinations, nested menus, long labels, or mobile layouts needing separate large tap targets. *Needs:* a display face with good punctuation, room for an unbroken line, and one clear current-item treatment.

- **Menu as display type.** The few nav items are the largest type on the first screen, 60–160px, so the menu is the hero. They are stacked one per line flush left in a serif or grotesque, or spread across the full width of the top or bottom edge in a condensed uppercase grotesque. The active item changes colour or style, and giant numerals can index a numbered set. *Fits:* expressive and humanistic studios, portfolios and small organisations with three to six one-word destinations. *Not:* long or multi-word labels, or pages that must lead with a message, product shot or call to action. *Needs:* few short destinations, a bold display face, and generous space around the words.

## Components

- **Capacity status beside the CTA.** A 6–8px dot, usually green, is followed by one short line giving remaining capacity and when it applies, such as a count of free slots and a month. It sits beside the main booking button in the nav or in a lower corner of the hero, as bare text in the dot's colour or a small outlined chip with the count. *Fits:* expressive studios, freelancers, agencies and booking-based services with a real capacity limit, and live products where liveness is real news. *Not:* status nobody keeps current, invented scarcity, pages already crowded with badges, or a bare dot that states nothing. *Needs:* a real, regularly updated state, and a specific line placed right at the primary action.

- **Command-line call to action.** The hero's single primary action is a fully rounded outlined pill about 280–320px wide and 40px tall, centred under the subhead. It holds a muted prompt glyph, a one-line install or scaffold command in monospace, and a copy icon at the right. Or put the same copyable command small in the header, or offer paste-ready agent prompts as mono blocks under short task headings, with optional package-manager tabs. *Fits:* technical frameworks, libraries, CLIs and agent-friendly tools, where running one command is the conversion. *Not:* non-developer audiences, products without a one-command install, or a competing sign-up button in the hero. *Needs:* a real command, a monospace face, copy-to-clipboard with brief confirmation, and a quiet hero.

- **Corner teaser card.** A small horizontal card, 16–24% of the viewport wide and 60–120px tall, is pinned to a lower corner of the first screen opposite the headline. A square thumbnail sits left. On the right are a tiny muted "latest" label or dot and a one- or two-line title. The whole card is one link on a tinted or contrasting panel. *Fits:* expressive and technical agencies, studios, energy, finance and product brands promoting a fresh case study, article, launch or video while the hero keeps a big wordmark or claim. *Not:* overlap with the headline or call to action on narrow screens (move or drop it), a close button or popup animation, nothing fresh to link, or text-only strips. *Needs:* a current item with a strong small thumbnail, and an empty lower corner.

- **Face in the contact button.** A small portrait, 28–48px, square with slightly rounded corners or circular, of the person you will actually speak to sits at the start of the main contact button, before a short label such as a call to book. It appears in the hero, at the nav's right end or in a fixed corner button. Or put a name, a role line and a green online dot between portrait and button. *Fits:* expressive and technical freelancers, small studios and service firms where clients hire specific people and the conversion is a call or chat. *Not:* large companies with no real named contact, stock faces, or a face that changes at random. *Needs:* a real, well-lit headshot cropped tight enough to read at thumbnail size.

- **Pasted label over the photo edge.** A solid caption block (a title, kicker plus headline, or square date badge) covers 10–25% of a photo's height at its top or bottom corner. It runs past the photo's side edge or leaves a strip of image beside it, so it reads as a label stuck on. It uses the page ground or one accent and can hold a tag, date or counter. *Fits:* expressive and humanistic editorial fronts, property and hospitality listings, collections and event lists that should feel curated. *Not:* labels covering faces or subjects, unpredictable crops from user uploads or mixed ratios, or weak images. *Needs:* art-directed photos with quiet corners, and one fixed rule for corner, overlap and colour, repeated on every card.

- **Ruled index table.** The archive is a full-width list with rows of 50–100px pitch, split by 1px edge-to-edge hairlines. Fixed columns hold a small number, the item name (body size up to 48px heavy display), then kind, credit and year in small type aligned right, with optional sortable headers, filter chips, a plus expander or hover thumbnail. Or make it a 40–50px ledger for dates, awards or contacts with a group year on the first row only and a right-aligned value; or an editorial index of 170–370px rows with 60–90px names and a description column at the page's midline. *Fits:* expressive portfolios, studios, film catalogues and archives of ten or more comparable entries. *Not:* image-led work with few projects, or mobile without collapsed side columns. *Needs:* consistent metadata per entry.

- **Ruled spec-sheet facts.** Product facts are set as label–value pairs. Labels are tiny bold uppercase sans of 11–13px. Values are the same size or slightly larger in mono, sans or serif. Pairs sit in 2–4 columns inside a grid of thin rules, or one per row with the value pushed right, optionally keyed by small coloured dots, with the product image allowed to cross the grid lines. *Fits:* expressive and technical products sold on exact attributes, such as dimensions, materials, maker, range, fees, ingredients and credits, including as a repeated strip per catalogue item. *Not:* slogans dressed as data, a lone pair posing as a sheet, or long values wrapping in narrow cells. *Needs:* 3–6 precise facts per item with consistent attribute names, and labels kept quieter than values.

- **Input as primary action.** The hero's main action is an input instead of a button. A field or textarea 470–745px wide has an inset send button, with pre-filled prefixes or suggestion chips beneath, so the user starts the product by typing. *Fits:* technical AI assistants, search, audits, generators and sign-ups where the first input is the product's real first step. *Not:* products whose first step isn't text, or simple sign-ups where typing up front adds friction. *Needs:* good starter suggestions, and a back end that answers the first input well.

- **Interactive pricing calculator.** Pricing is driven by visitor input. Sliders, dials, dropdowns or radio rows for usage (list size, pageviews, seats) update prices live above a single checkout button. Or pair sliders with one large live figure, 100px or more or in a display serif, showing price or savings, with the breakdown and call to action or email capture right under it. *Fits:* technical usage-based or volume-tiered products where the right plan depends on the buyer's numbers. *Not:* simple flat pricing, where the calculator adds work for nothing. *Needs:* a real pricing formula, and sensible default values.

- **Sample-data preview as empty state.** An empty screen shows ghosted placeholder rows or a faded miniature of the filled screen above its message, previewing what will appear. Or offer real next steps instead of a lone illustration: install snippets with tabs, a config block, tiles shaped like the future items, or a grid of linked service-type cards. *Fits:* technical lists, dashboards and onboarding screens where users should see what to expect before adding data. *Not:* ghosts that could be mistaken for real data. *Needs:* a clear first action next to the preview.

- **Text-only call to action.** The call to action is a styled word rather than a filled button. It can be a 12px wide-tracked uppercase word with a 1px underline, a word between thin ornamental rules with small end ornaments, or one oversized word with a pointing glyph pinned to a corner. No filled button appears anywhere on the page. *Fits:* expressive and humanistic editorial, luxury, literary and heritage pages where typography carries the brand and actions are few. *Not:* conversion-heavy commerce or apps where users scan for a button shape, or low-contrast pages where a bare word disappears. *Needs:* a refined face with good caps and figures, and one or two actions at most.

## Interaction and motion

- **Dimmed list, one lit item.** A long list is set at 24–40px, all in a pale grey at about 40–55% contrast. Only the active item turns full dark or white, sometimes with a 3–4px side bar, and opens its copy, thumbnail or card beside the list. Scroll, hover or auto-cycling moves the lit item; in a scrolling reel the lit row stays on a fixed centre line. *Fits:* expressive, humanistic and technical feature sequences, use-case rosters, project indexes and numbered tabs, where the list's size matters but one item holds focus. *Not:* dimmed items below readable contrast, items that must be compared side by side, or touch screens without a scroll or tap fallback. *Needs:* 3 to 20 or more short labels, each with its own content.

- **Bright-rule link underline.** Inline links keep the body text colour or step only to full black, with a solid 2–3px underline in one saturated accent, offset 2–4px below the baseline. On a neutral page that rule is the only bright colour in the text. A quieter version uses a thin offset rule in a tinted grey. *Fits:* expressive and humanistic essays, personal sites, editorial and podcast home pages with frequent inline links. *Not:* pale hairlines that vanish at small sizes, accents too light on white, or pages using the accent elsewhere for decoration. *Needs:* a mostly neutral palette, and line height enough for the offset rule to clear descenders.

- **Sentence-shaped form.** A form's few inputs sit inline in one first-person sentence at running-text size. Each field looks like a lightly boxed or underlined word (a date, a quantity, a plan) filling a blank, for example a request to arrive on one date and stay until another. The sentence is centred in its own band, with one submit button below. Or let a headline's single noun be swappable to change the content shown beneath. *Fits:* expressive, humanistic or technical bookings, subscriptions, sign-ups and quick configurators with 1–3 variables. *Not:* over three fields, inputs needing validation or help text, or wraps that strand a field from its words. *Needs:* a natural sentence that reads right with defaults filled in, native inputs with accessible labels, and fields sized like text.

- **Working product slice.** Where the hero or second-screen screenshot would go, place a real interactive miniature of the product, framed in its native setting (a window, panel, board or terminal), at 60–85% of content width. Add a one-line hint or arrow inviting a try and optionally a score or result beside it. The interaction should finish in seconds and show the core benefit directly. *Fits:* humanistic, technical and expressive utilities, games, learning apps and developer tools whose value is felt in one gesture. *Not:* demos that are heavy, slow to start, break on touch or small screens, behave unlike the shipped product, or need long setup or real data. *Needs:* a self-contained front-end component with mock data, a static fallback, and a short invitation line.

- **Pinned scroll stage.** The viewport pins while scroll scrubs one centred object, 30–50% of the viewport, through its rotations, states or a sequence of items, on an empty flat field. Only small fixed labels sit at the edges, sometimes with a large index numeral that ticks as you scroll. *Fits:* expressive, humanistic or technical hardware and product launches, catalogues swapping one item at a time, and narrative pieces where one object deserves the screen. *Not:* content-heavy or task pages, long reading, slow devices or weak assets. Always show a scroll cue, progress and a way to skip, since it overrides normal scrolling. *Needs:* scroll-linked animation, and a high-quality render, 3D model or image sequence.

- **Frozen mid-interaction mockup.** A product mockup is caught mid-action. A selected element has a 1px blue outline, a name tag and a floating rail of action icons, or a card is lifted with a shadow, an 8° tilt and a grab cursor over a hatched drop slot, sometimes breaking the frame's border. *Fits:* technical editors, builders and drag-and-drop tools, where the moment of manipulation explains the product faster than a static screen. *Not:* products whose value isn't direct manipulation, or too many staged states in one mockup, which looks fake. *Needs:* a carefully staged mockup or rebuilt interface showing one believable action.

## Texture

- **Exposed column guides.** Draw 1px dashed or solid vertical hairlines at 6–10% contrast at every column boundary of a 4–5 column grid, about 270px apart in a 1080–1100px container. They run from below the nav to the footer and are crossed by full-width section rules. Card edges, headline starts and stats sit exactly on them, with optional square nodes at crossings or a short coloured tick where a stat starts. *Fits:* technical infrastructure, fintech and developer products, and expressive or humanistic studios, essays and architecture firms that want an engineered look on light, airy layouts. *Not:* warm or organic brands, free-form or centred compositions, or dense and dark pages where lines vanish or clutter. *Needs:* a real fixed grid that every section follows, lines lighter than body text, and exact alignment.

- **Hand-drawn marks on precise type.** Put 2–4 rough single-colour pen or crayon marks on clean sans type in a strict layout. Options are a wavy underline as wide as the word and 6–12px tall, an oval or burst of 8–12 dashes ringing a name with 20–40px clearance, a marker swipe under a script eyebrow, or small doodles on photos. Or annotate a sober proof: a brush underline, a loop round one photo, a 100–150px curved arrow, a margin note, at most one to three per screen. *Fits:* expressive and technical portfolios, event series, venues, journalism and friendly B2B products wanting a human voice. *Not:* formal, luxury, legal or medical contexts, marks covering letters, marks on every heading, or mixed colours and strokes. *Needs:* textured SVG strokes sized to their words, a crisp sans, and one accent colour.

- **Hard offset shadow.** Key elements get a 1–2px dark outline and a solid, unblurred copy of their shape offset 4–8px down and right, so they read as stickers or pressed keys. Use it on pill CTAs, product mockups, feature cards, even headline words or tilted banners, with the shadow pure black or a darker tone of the ground. *Fits:* expressive creator marketplaces, agencies, festivals, podcasts and studios wanting cartoon or neo-brutalist tactility with flat colour and chunky type. *Not:* calm, premium or clinical products, dense data UIs, or every card on the page. Keep it to CTAs and a few hero objects. *Needs:* flat fills, one outline weight, one fixed offset direction and distance, and a light or tinted ground.

- **Registration crosshair marks.** Small plus-sign crosshairs, 12–24px with a 1–2px stroke in the text colour or a low-contrast tone, sit as a mirrored pair at a section's top corners, at the seam where a full-bleed block meets the next, or in the margin beside an inset hero card. The page reads as a print sheet or viewfinder. Tiny solid squares as label bullets can echo them. *Fits:* expressive studios, production companies, type foundries and agencies, and technical hardware or engineering products on stark, generous-margin layouts. *Not:* warm, caring or playful brands, marks on every card, or any mark that could pass for an add or expand control. *Needs:* a strict grid with clear seams or an inset frame, margin space, and one mark size and stroke.

- **Tilted paper-strip labels.** Short labels such as nav items, a tagline or list entries each sit on their own off-white strip. Strips have 6–12px padding, light paper grain and square corners, and are rotated −3° to +3° so neighbours don't line up. They stack into a loose, slightly overlapping list or a row of nav on a tinted or dark ground. *Fits:* humanistic friendly, hand-made, food, course, newsletter and community brands wanting a scrapbook feel, especially beside cut-out imagery. *Not:* serious, financial or technical products, anything longer than one line, data or form fields, or tilting everything. Keep body copy and main buttons straight. *Needs:* a ground that contrasts with the strips, optional scan texture, and a typewriter or hand-lettered face on some strips.
